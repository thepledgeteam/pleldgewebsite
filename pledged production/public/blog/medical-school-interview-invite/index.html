You’ve Received a Medical School Interview Invite—Now What?

Published on: September 2025 • 7 min read

Receiving a medical school interview invitation is a major milestone. It typically means the admissions committee sees you as a strong candidate and wants to evaluate you beyond your grades and test scores.

After the well deserved excitement settles, given the work it takes to reach this stage, many applicants are left asking the same question:

Now what should I do to prepare for my medical school interview?

This post walks you through exactly that.

While I use the University of Toronto medical school interview as an example, the principles discussed apply broadly to Canadian medical schools and, with slight variations, to U.S. medical schools as well. These insights are based on personal experience interviewing at institutions such as Harvard, Stanford, Cleveland Clinic Lerner College of Medicine, and Washington University in St. Louis.

What Medical Schools Are Really Assessing in Interviews

To prepare effectively, you must first understand what medical school interviewers are looking for.

CanMEDS Framework (Canada)

In Canada, most medical schools assess applicants using the CanMEDS competency framework, developed by the Royal College of Physicians and Surgeons of Canada. This framework underpins physician training and evaluation nationwide.

ACGME Core Competencies (United States)

In the U.S., the equivalent model is the ACGME Core Competencies. While the two frameworks are very similar, CanMEDS places an emphasis on health advocacy, which has no direct American counterpart.

Key Competencies Assessed in Medical School Interviews

  • Medical Expert (U.S.: Medical Knowledge)
  • Communicator (Interpersonal and Communication Skills)
  • Collaborator (Interpersonal Skills & Patient Care)
  • Leader/Manager (Systems-Based Practice)
  • Health Advocate (primarily Canadian schools)
  • Scholar (Practice-Based Learning and Improvement)
  • Professional (Professionalism)

A common misconception is that applicants should only be evaluated on academics. In reality, medical school interviews focus heavily on professionalism, ethics, teamwork, communication, and self-reflection.

“I’m Not a Medical Student Yet—Why Am I Being Assessed This Way?”

If a school has invited you to interview, it has likely already identified early evidence of these competencies in your application. The interview is their opportunity to see how you think, communicate, and reflect in real time. In other words, they are not expecting perfection—they are looking for potential.

Types of Medical School Interviews

Most medical school interviews fall into one of the following formats:

1. Traditional Medical School Interviews

  • One-on-one or panel-based
  • Conversational or structured
  • Often focused on motivation, ethics, and experiences

2. Multiple Mini Interviews (MMI)

MMIs consist of several short stations designed to assess different competencies.

University of Toronto’s Modified Personal Interview (MPI)

  • Four independent interview stations
  • Each assessed by a different interviewer
  • Typically 12 minutes per station

The University of Toronto is transparent about assessing applicants across four domains:

  • Professionalism
  • Communicator / Collaborator / Leader
  • Health Advocacy
  • Scholarship

These domains closely align with the CanMEDS framework.

How to Answer Medical School Interview Questions Effectively

To maximize your chances of acceptance, your interview answers should intentionally demonstrate multiple competencies.

Example: “What Is Your Greatest Achievement?”

Most applicants have several achievements they could discuss. A strategic approach is to choose an experience that highlights multiple CanMEDS or ACGME competencies at once. For example, you might describe:

  • Leading or collaborating with a team
  • Identifying a real problem in your community
  • Taking initiative to address it
  • Reflecting on what you learned

A community-based research or volunteer project can demonstrate collaboration, advocacy, professionalism, and scholarship. What matters most is how you reflect on the experience, not how impressive it sounds on paper.

Medical School Interview Preparation: A Word of Caution

Effective medical school interview preparation does not need to be expensive. Once you understand what schools are assessing, you can gain significant value from:

  • Practicing with family or friends
  • Recording and reviewing your responses
  • Seeking honest, constructive feedback

While coaching can be helpful, you do not need to pay exorbitant fees to succeed. Often, a single focused session with an experienced admissions coach is enough to help you develop a clear interview strategy.

Final Thoughts

A medical school interview invite means you are already a competitive applicant. Your goal now is to show admissions committees who you are, how you think, and how you will grow into the role of a physician. Preparation is not about memorizing answers, it’s about understanding the framework, reflecting deeply on your experiences, and communicating with clarity and authenticity.
